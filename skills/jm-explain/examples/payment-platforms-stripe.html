How payment platforms like Stripe and Polar work

A payment platform lets a business take money from a customer's card through one programming interface, an API: requests the business's own server sends to the platform's servers. The customer types a card number, the business gets a yes within a few seconds, and the money reaches the business's bank account days later.2 Stripe alone moved $1.4T in 2024, about 1.3% of global GDP.1

Behind that one interface sit organisations that never deal with a small business directly. The customer's bank decides whether to pay, a card network such as Visa or Mastercard carries the request between banks, and a bank on the side of the merchant, the business doing the selling, receives the money.2 Taking cards used to mean opening your own account with that bank, passing its checks and doing the security and fraud work yourself. A platform replaces that with one account it holds on behalf of every business that signs up.3

Platforms come in two kinds. Stripe is a payment service provider: it moves the money, while the business stays the seller and carries the tax and other liabilities of each sale.17 Polar is a merchant of record: built on Stripe, it takes on those liabilities, sales tax first among them, by becoming the seller.17 The figure shows the parties every card payment crosses; the eight sections below follow one payment through them, from signup to tax.

Before you start

00
Issuer
The customer's bank, the one that gave out the card. It approves or declines each payment and holds the customer's money or credit line.2
Card network
Visa, Mastercard and their peers, which carry requests and money between banks.2 They also set the fees the banks on the network charge each other.14
Acquirer
The bank on the merchant's side. It receives card payments for the merchant and routes them through the network to the issuer.2
Authorize, capture
Authorizing asks the issuer to approve an amount and hold it on the card; capturing tells it to take the held money. The two can happen together or days apart.12
Merchant of record
The business legally named as the seller. It owes the sales tax on each sale and carries the liabilities that come with selling.17

One account carries millions of businesses

01

Traditionally each business that takes cards gets its own merchant account at an acquirer, after the bank has reviewed it. A payment facilitator, which is what Stripe is, registers one master merchant account with an acquiring bank and signs businesses up beneath it as sub-merchants.3 Signing up is a web form. Behind it the platform verifies the owner's identity, checks sanctions lists and judges the business's financial health and risk.3

The slow part is done once. To process for sub-merchants, a platform has to approach an acquirer with a business plan and win its sponsorship, which takes 3 to 6 months at minimum.3 Paying that cost once and spreading it over every sub-merchant is what lets a new business take its first card payment on the day it signs up, instead of after its own bank review.

The catch is that the platform answers for everyone under its account. It must monitor sub-merchants against the networks' rules, block or review suspicious payments, and submit evidence on their behalf when their customers dispute a charge.3 So it keeps checking after signup: Stripe typically schedules a new account's first payout 7 to 14 days after its first real payment, depending on industry, country and risk level.13 When a sub-merchant suddenly looks risky, holding its balance is how the platform avoids paying for that sub-merchant's losses itself, which is why "Stripe froze my account" stories exist.

The card number goes around the merchant

02

The payment form is the platform's code, not the merchant's. Stripe's hosted Checkout page, or its Elements fields placed inside the merchant's page, send the card details from the customer's browser to Stripe's vault, its locked-down card storage, without passing through the merchant's servers.4 What comes back is safe to keep: an ID for the stored card, plus its type, last four digits and expiry date, none of which falls under the card security rules.4 The merchant's server charges that ID, a token standing in for the card, and never handles the number itself.

The reason is the cost of touching a card number. Anyone who stores, processes or transmits card data must comply with PCI DSS, the card industry's security standard.4 A business that handles raw card numbers can face more than 300 security controls, dedicated security hardware and outside auditors every year.4 Routing the number around the merchant moves that burden onto Stripe, which an independent assessor certifies every year at the strictest level, PCI Level 1.4

The form is only as safe as the page around it. Every script the page loads from another site can run arbitrary code there if that site is compromised, so Stripe advises keeping such scripts off payment pages.4 And a business that builds its own form and posts card numbers to its own server is back inside the full standard, whichever platform it uses.4

Each payment is a record that changes state

03

On Stripe a payment is an object, the PaymentIntent, which the merchant's server creates as soon as it knows the amount, so that every attempt to pay it is recorded.5 It moves through a short list of states: waiting for a payment method, sometimes waiting for the customer to pass an extra check, processing, and succeeded. A decline sends it back to the first state, so the customer can try another card.5

It is built as a record because a payment is a conversation, not one request. The customer's bank may ask for an extra check, and bank debits, payments pulled straight from a bank account rather than a card, can take several days to confirm.5 A single "charge this card" call that answers yes or no has no way to say "waiting for the customer"; a record with a status does, and the merchant only has to read it.

Two failures shape how merchants talk to this record. Connections drop, and a server that resends "create payment" without knowing whether the first request landed can charge twice. The fix is an idempotency key, a random ID the merchant attaches to the request: a retry with the same key gets the saved first response instead of a second payment.6 Keys can be cleared after 24 hours, so a much later retry counts as a new request.6

The second failure is missing news. Outcomes that arrive later reach the merchant as webhooks, web requests from Stripe to an address on the merchant's server, sent when a bank confirms a payment or a customer disputes one.7 Stripe retries a failed delivery for up to three days, may deliver an event twice and doesn't keep events in order, so the merchant's handler must skip event IDs it has already processed.7 A shop that fulfils orders only when the customer's browser returns to its thank-you page loses every order whose customer closed the tab first.

Risk is scored before the bank is asked

04

Before a payment goes to the customer's bank, Radar, Stripe's fraud system, gives it a risk score from 0 to 99: the probability that it ends in a fraud dispute or a fraud warning from the bank.8 A default rule blocks payments at the highest risk level.8 A blocked payment is never sent to the issuer for authorization.10 An elevated score is a prompt to review the payment or ask for more verification, not a verdict that it is fraud.8

The platform does this rather than leaving it to each shop because it sees what no single shop can. Radar's models train on data from millions of businesses.10 A card or device that failed at one shop an hour ago is therefore a signal at the next one. Blocking before authorization also stops a payment that might have turned into a dispute later.10

Every threshold trades fraud for lost sales: block more, and more good customers are turned away. The sharpest attack is card testing, where scripts push many small payments with stolen card numbers through a shop to learn which cards still work, then use or resell the good ones.9 A leaked secret API key, the password the merchant's server uses to call Stripe, lets testers do it through the merchant's own account.9 When Stripe detects an account under fraud pressure, it blocks more elevated-risk payments for a while and returns to the normal threshold once the threat passes.8

The customer's bank says yes or no

05

The platform sends an authorization request through the card network to the issuer.2 The issuer checks that the card is valid and not reported lost or stolen, that the account has the funds or credit, and that the details match its records, and the answer comes back the same way, usually within a few seconds.2 On a yes, the customer's available balance drops at once, although no money has moved yet.2

Only the issuer can decide, because only it knows the customer's account: its own models weigh spending habits, the balance and the card details on every request.10 The network in the middle lets any acquirer reach any issuer over one connection, instead of each bank linking to every other bank. In the European Economic Area a second rule applies. Since 14 September 2019, Strong Customer Authentication has required online card payments to use 3D Secure, a step where the bank asks the customer to prove it is really them.11 In return, 3D Secure moves the liability for a fraudulent payment off the merchant.5

A decline comes back as a short code: insufficient funds, a lost or stolen card, or no specific reason at all.10 The issuer's models are its own, so a merchant can watch its decline rate rise without ever learning why. In Europe, a bank may also decline a payment that skipped the customer check when one was required.11

The money moves later, minus fees

06

Capture turns the hold into a charge. Most payments are captured at once, but a hotel can authorize at booking and capture at checkout.12 Captured payments then settle: the money itself moves from the issuer to the merchant's side.2 On the way, the acquirer pays the issuer an interchange fee, set by the card networks and averaging about 2% of the sale in the US.14 The platform credits what is left, minus its own fee, to the merchant's balance and pays it out to the merchant's bank on a schedule.13

Approval and money are split because they need different speeds. The approval has to happen while the customer waits at checkout; the money can travel in bulk transfers between banks, which is cheaper than moving it one payment at a time. The split also lets a merchant capture only what it actually ships.12 With daily payouts and Stripe's standard US timing, a payment is paid out 2 business days after it is captured.13

A hold doesn't last. Online card authorizations typically expire after 7 days; if the merchant hasn't captured by then, the funds are released and the payment is cancelled.12 Fees also change with place: the EU caps interchange on personal cards at 0.3% for credit and 0.2% for debit, so the same sale costs a different amount to accept in Europe than in the US.14

Disputes pull the money back

07

A customer who contests a payment does it with their own bank, not with the merchant. The issuer starts a chargeback, a reversal through the card network: the network debits Stripe, and Stripe debits the merchant's balance for the amount plus a dispute fee.15 The merchant then has 7 to 21 days to send evidence, and the issuer takes 60 to 75 days to decide.15 The decision is the issuer's alone; Stripe has no say in it.15

The rules favour the cardholder. Customers can usually dispute up to 120 days after paying, and for something paid in advance, such as a ticket, the window starts on the event date.15 That protection is part of why people type their card number into shops they have never used before. The network takes the money from Stripe, not from the merchant, and Stripe recovers it from the merchant's balance straight away.15

The industry treats dispute activity above 0.75% of payments as excessive, and disputes count whether they are won or lost.16 A merchant above that line for months can land in the networks' monitoring programs, pay fines and lose the ability to take cards with other payment companies too.16 If a merchant's balance can't cover a lost dispute, the platform pays the network itself, which is the real reason behind the held payouts in step 1.

A merchant of record becomes the seller

08

Polar adds one more layer on top. As merchant of record it takes on the international sales taxes of each sale: it registers, files and pays them in the places where it is registered, so the developer doesn't track where tax is owed.17 It calculates the tax with Stripe Tax, Stripe's tax calculation service, and the payments themselves run on Stripe.17 The developer gets a payout minus Polar's fee, from 5% + 50¢ per sale on the free plan down to 3.4% + 30¢ on the $400-a-month plan, plus 1.5% for cards from outside the US.18

The reason is that selling software online creates tax duties wherever the customers live. A business outside the EU that sells services to EU consumers can register for VAT, the EU's sales tax, in a single member state under the One Stop Shop instead of in each one.20 It files one return there and pays that one state the VAT due across the EU.19 In the US, the Supreme Court's 2018 Wayfair ruling let states tax sales by sellers with no physical presence; South Dakota's threshold was $100,000 in sales or 200 transactions.21 A merchant of record registers and files once for all its sellers, which a two-person company could not afford to do in dozens of places.

The convenience costs money and control. Polar's own comparison lists higher fees per payment and less flexibility for advanced business and pricing models.17 The developer still owes income tax on its revenue at home.17 Disputes still happen, at $15 each whatever the outcome.18 And because Polar is the one legally selling the product, Polar decides which products it is willing to sell.

What it comes down to

09

One idea carries the whole system: a payment platform is a licensed middleman that turns a slow, bank-to-bank process into one record behind an API, and earns its fee by carrying risk. It carries the security risk with tokens, the fraud risk with scores, the credit risk by paying out before disputes close and, as a merchant of record, the tax risk.

Three of its patterns transfer to other systems:

  1. Aggregate under one licence. One party that passed the hard checks carries many small ones, and then has to watch them, as app stores and marketplaces do.
  2. Decide fast, settle slow. Approve while the user waits, and move the expensive thing in batches later.
  3. Make retries safe. Idempotency keys on the way in6, and events that may repeat, de-duplicated by ID on the way out.7

Going deeper. The main variants are the two this page compares, payment service providers where you stay the seller and merchants of record where they do, plus marketplace setups in which one platform collects payments for many sellers and splits them. Three numbers matter in practice: interchange of about 2% in the US against capped 0.2 to 0.3% in the EU14, dispute activity kept under 0.75%16, and authorizations that last about 7 days.12 Read Stripe's guide to payment facilitators next, then its page on how disputes work, then Polar's merchant of record page (sources 3, 15 and 17).

Limits. Stripe doesn't publish how it routes a payment between acquiring banks or what its fraud models weigh, so this page explains the documented architecture of such a platform, and sentences marked ~ are reconstruction. Bank transfers, wallets such as Apple Pay, subscriptions, in-person card readers and the history of the networks are left out. The fees and timings quoted are published defaults; payout timing varies by country and account.13
