How a card tap becomes money in the shop's account

A card payment lets a shop take money from your bank account and get an answer before you put the card away. It does this with two separate flows. In the first, a message asks your bank whether it will pay, say, €24.50, and the answer comes back in about 1 to 2 seconds.5 In the second, which runs in batches over the next 1 to 3 business days, the money itself moves.8

The split exists because the two jobs want different things: the shop needs a fast, reliable yes before it hands over the goods, while moving money between banks is cheaper in daily totals than one purchase at a time. So the yes at the till is a promise from your bank, and the rest of the system is how that promise gets kept.

Before you start

00
Issuer
Your bank, the one that issued your card.6 It checks your funds and approves or declines each payment.6
Acquirer
The shop's bank for card payments. It collects the shop's card sales and submits them to the card network.8
Card network
Visa, Mastercard and the like: the network that carries a card brand's transactions between the banks.6
Hold
The amount your bank sets aside after it says yes, guaranteed to the shop, though no money has moved yet.6
Clearing, settlement
Clearing is the exchange of the final sale records after the purchase.8 Settlement is when money actually moves between banks.8
Interchange
A fee your bank keeps from each card purchase.9 In the EU it is capped at 0.2% of the amount for consumer debit cards and 0.3% for consumer credit cards.9

The tap hands over a one-time code

01

The card reader puts out a 13.56 MHz radio field, the one the contactless card standard ISO/IEC 14443 defines, and the chip in your card answers through it.1 The reader gives the chip the amount and a fresh random number. The chip mixes them with a counter it raises on every payment and, with a secret key that only it and your bank hold, computes a short one-time code called the cryptogram.2 Because the counter and the random number change on every tap, a recorded code cannot be reused for another purchase.2

Your bank is asked, and says yes

02

The reader sends the card details and the cryptogram to the shop's bank, which wraps them in a standard card message, ISO 8583 type 0100, an authorization request.3 The card network forwards it to your bank.6 Your bank checks the cryptogram against its copy of the key,2 checks your funds and puts a hold on the amount.6 It replies with a 0110 message carrying response code 00, approved.3 The reply retraces the path, and the reader shows "approved", usually 1 to 2 seconds after the tap.5 If the shop never follows up, the hold lapses, after about 5 days for a Visa payment made in person.7

That night, the shop's bank sends the receipts

03

An approval is not yet a payment: in this design the request posts nothing to your account, and a separate file sent later turns the approval into a charge.3 At the end of the day the shop's card sales go into one batch, and the shop's bank sends them to the network as a clearing file, on business days.4 The network sorts the records by bank, and your bank matches its record to the hold by the six-character approval code from its reply, then posts the charge. Clearing usually completes within hours of the batch arriving.8

The network nets, then the money moves

04

Settlement moves money in daily totals, not per purchase. Your bank pays what it owes for the day to the card network, which nets the amounts, meaning it offsets what each bank owes against what it is owed, so each shop's bank receives one sum for the day.8 On the way your bank keeps the interchange: at most 0.2% of €24.50 under the EU debit cap, about €0.05.9 The network also charges the shop's bank a fee, and the shop's bank takes a margin; neither is published per payment, shown here as s and m.

The shop gets paid on a schedule

05

Settlement between the banks takes 1 to 3 business days, and the shop's bank then pays the shop on a set payout schedule that need not line up with it: many providers smooth the timing into a fixed payout calendar.8 For the shop, your €24.50 arrives inside one lump deposit for the day's card sales, typically a day or two after the tap.

What it comes down to

06

One idea carries the whole system: promise now, pay later, in bulk. The tap gets a promise from your bank in about two seconds, protected by a code that works once. The money follows in daily batches, netted between banks, and reaches the shop on its bank's schedule.

The same split works wherever a quick yes and a slow, costly transfer have to live together:

  1. Prove the request is fresh. A code built from a counter and a random challenge beats a static secret that can be copied.
  2. Reserve, don't move. A hold sets the money aside and can lapse, so nothing irreversible happens at the till.
  3. Confirm separately. A later record, tied to the approval, turns the reservation into a charge.
  4. Settle net. Add everything up per party and move one sum a day.

Limits. This page follows a physical card tapped at a shop. Phone wallets send a stand-in number instead of the card number, online payments add an identity check with your bank, and some debit payments use a single 0200 message that asks and posts at once.3 Refunds, disputes and how fees are set are left out.
