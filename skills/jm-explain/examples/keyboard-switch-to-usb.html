How a mechanical key switch closes a circuit, and how that press reaches the computer

A keyboard has one job: tell the computer, within a few milliseconds (ms, thousandths of a second), which keys are held down. Each key sits on a mechanical switch, a spring-loaded plastic plunger that pushes two metal strips together partway down its travel.1 A small chip inside the keyboard notices which switch closed, checks that the signal is real and sends a key number to the computer over the USB cable.3

Two facts make this more than one wire per key. Metal strips that meet rattle for a few milliseconds before they settle, and over USB the computer asks for news instead of waiting to be told.2, 8 So a press passes through four stages before the computer reads key number 4, the A key.

Before you start

00
Firmware
The keyboard's chip is a microcontroller: a tiny computer whose metal legs, called pins, it can switch on or read. The program it runs is the firmware; this page uses the names and defaults of QMK, an open-source keyboard firmware.5
Actuation point
The depth at which a switch registers a press: 2 mm on a Cherry MX Red, a common switch model.1
Bounce
Two metal contacts that meet touch, part and touch again several times before they settle. Cherry rates the MX Red's bounce time at 5 ms.2
Matrix
A grid of row wires and column wires with one switch at each crossing, so the chip needs a pin per row and per column instead of one per key.3
Key code
The number the USB standard gives each key position, written in hexadecimal (base 16) with a 0x prefix: 0x04 is 4, the key marked A.6
Polling
Over USB the computer asks each device for news at a fixed interval, and a device only answers when asked.8

A plunger holds two metal leaves apart

01

The switch has five parts: a top housing that guides the stem, the plastic plunger the keycap clips onto; a coil spring that pushes the stem back up; and a bottom housing that holds the contacts, two gold-plated metal strips or leaves, rated for more than 100 million presses.1 Their ends leave the bottom as two pins soldered to the keyboard's circuit board. At rest a leg on the stem holds the moving leaf off the fixed one.

The circuit closes halfway down

02

Pressing squeezes the spring, so the force needed rises from 30 cN at rest. At 2 mm down, with 45 cN on the key, the circuit closes.1 A centinewton (cN) is about the weight of one gram, so that is roughly a 46 g weight. The stem stops at 4 mm, where the spring pushes back with 100 cN.1 Cherry calls the Red linear: the force climbs with no bump or click along the way.1

The chip finds the key in a grid

03

A full keyboard has about 100 switches and its chip far fewer pins, so the switches sit where row wires cross column wires.3 A 6 by 18 grid covers 108 keys with 24 pins. The firmware puts voltage on one column, reads every row at once, then moves on.3 A closed switch connects its column to its row, so that row reads 1 and the pair names the key.3 A diode on each switch, a part that lets current flow one way only, stops three held keys from faking a fourth.3

It waits for the leaves to stop bouncing

04

The grid is read far faster than the leaves settle, so raw readings would show one press as several. QMK's default filter starts a timer at any change and restarts it at every further change; it only accepts the new state after 5 ms with no change.4 The cost is delay: the key counts as pressed 5 ms after the last rattle, not at the first touch.

The computer collects the key code when it asks

05

The firmware looks up row 1, column 0 in its keymap, the table from grid position to key code, and gets 0x04.6 It writes this into an 8-byte report, a message of eight small numbers: the first holds Shift, Ctrl, Alt and the Windows or Command key as on and off flags, the second is unused, and the other six hold up to six key codes.7

The keyboard cannot send the report on its own. When plugged in it tells the computer how often to ask, as often as every 1 ms.8 On each poll it answers "nothing new" or hands over the new report. The computer sees code 0x04 appear and turns it into the letter a for the app in front.

What it comes down to

06

One idea carries the whole path: a switch closes a contact at a fixed depth, and everything after it turns "row 1, column 0 closed" into "key 4 held" and waits to be asked.

The same moves show up well beyond keyboards:

  1. Scan a grid to save pins. Power one line, read the others; LED panels and phone keypads do the same.
  2. Wait for quiet before trusting a contact. Any button or mechanical switch needs a few milliseconds of no change.
  3. Send state, not events. A report of what is held now lets the receiver work out the changes, and a lost report corrects itself on the next.

Limits. The numbers are Cherry's ratings for the MX Red and QMK's defaults; other switches and firmware differ. Switches with a bump or a click, magnetic and optical switches, Bluetooth and the computer's own input handling are left out. The bounce pattern in Fig. 5 is illustrative.
